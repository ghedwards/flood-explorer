Simulates water moving over terrain. Constant inputs for this run; no automatic conversion from gauge height to flow. Switch back to the fast model to use gauge-level, storm and defence controls.

Rainfall is runoff after losses. Unmapped boundaries and missing terrain are closed walls. Terrain is averaged into blocks; narrow channels, drawn defences, pipes and bathymetry are not represented. Results are final depths, not peak depths. Runs are not restored after reload or included in share links.

Storm tide = tide + surge + wave setup, in metres above sea level (AHD). The surge buttons are examples: real surge depends on the cyclone's track, speed and the shape of the coast and seabed. Queensland's mapped storm tide hazard areas are in Layers.

Flood over time
Dam release upstream

Choose a smooth rise and fall, or a recorded flood at a nearby gauge (hour-by-hour readings from Water Data Online). The flood reaches places upstream of the gauge earlier and downstream later. Routed sends it down the river below the gauge (Muskingum–Cunge): flat, deep reaches spread and lower the peak, and tributaries joining below the gauge add their own flood, which usually peaks sooner. On the coast the sea can rise and fall with the tide, with the bay level as high water. A screening sketch: real floods depend on rainfall timing, storage and dam releases.

Levees and flood gates

Levees are raised to their crest in the terrain (from LiDAR, a mapped height, or the height you set); a failed levee or a breach drops to the ground beside it, and water that gets past a levee fills the land behind to the river's level. Closed flood gates stop the river backing up the drains behind them. Mapping of levees and gates in OpenStreetMap is patchy: draw any that are missing.

About this map

Set a river level at the gauge and a bay level, and the map shows land below that water surface that connects to the river. It can add storms, levees, tides and the flood's timing, but all simply: it is a screening tool, not a flood study, and leaves out bridges, culverts and detailed flow. For real flood risk use your council's flood maps (for example Brisbane's Flood Awareness Maps) and Bureau of Meteorology warnings.
